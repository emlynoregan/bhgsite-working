The Burra Boys: Rob Zacher, Don Griffen and a Musical History of Burra

  • Status: background research dossier; not a finished public-history article.
  • Prepared: 1 October 2026, from photographs supplied by Emlyn O'Regan in conversation.
  • Principal objects: the LP sleeve of The Burra Boys, its printed/hand-lettered lyric sheets, a copy of a Burra Broadcaster article (12 July 2000), and associated handwritten research notes.
  • Companion document: working lyric transcriptions of all twelve songs (planning repository only; not published here).
  • Related research: Johnny Green, Elizabeth A. Ward, and the BHG site article inventory.
  • Editorial rule: distinguish statements on an original sleeve or lyric sheet from a newspaper report, a later handwritten lead, and our own interpretation. Neither the recorded performances nor outside biographical or discographic databases have yet been checked.

Why this matters

The Burra Boys is an unusually concentrated piece of late-twentieth-century local memory. Rob Zacher and Don Griffen wrote songs about Burra's copper mine, the lives of miners and workers, local landmarks, drink and humour, the pastoral country, and Burra people who went to the First World War. The surviving lyric sheet dates individual songs to 1979–81, while the record sleeve carries a 1982 copyright notice; a contemporary report establishes that it was publicly launched on 8 April 1983. The performers were therefore revisiting the nineteenth-century mining town from the vantage point of the Burra district in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

The songs are valuable as evidence of how local history was remembered and performed, not automatically as eyewitness evidence for the 1840s–1870s or the First World War. The singer's persona, an evocative date, or a witty lyric may be a dramatic construction. For factual history, compare the stories with contemporary newspapers, the Ian Auhl Collection, the mine-history records, and other primary material. For the history of the songs themselves, the sleeve, lyric sheets, recordings and participants' testimony are the relevant primary sources.

The seven original photographed JPEGs are now preserved byte-for-byte in the source-photo inventory, verified in the repository on 2 October 2026. These photographs are not flat archival scans. Obtain clean scans of both sleeve faces, actual disc labels, every entire lyric page and the newspaper copy alongside any eventual archive publication. Composition, recordings and dating

The photographed lyric handout credits songs individually and prints dates ranging from 1979 to 1981. That is evidence for attributed compositions or their dating on the handout; it does not establish a precise date of recording, pressing, release, concert performance or copyright transfer. The sleeve's © 1982 dates its copyright. Contemporary local reporting on 31 March 1983 announced its release at the Adelaide National Folk Festival; on 7 April the paper reported that copies were not yet ready, and an account dated 14 April dates the official release to the Paxton Square restoration opening on Friday 8 April 1983. A handwritten note mentions a concert dated 4 December 1982; a period concert notice independently announces a 4 December Burra Town Hall performance, but that notice's year has not yet been verified from the print original.

Musical personnel and instrumentation

The lyric sheets carry individual performer credits, rather than a single uniform album line-up. They repeatedly name Rob Zacher and Don Griffen as singers, guitarists and arrangers and name Don on violin or harmony for some songs. “Redruth Jail – Aberdeen Wail” names Rob as guitarist/lead and Don on violin/harmony; “Morphett Pumphouse” names Don on drums; “Thomas Pickett” includes Rob on pan pipes. The detailed allocations should be extracted song by song from sharper scans before publishing a sessionography. The sleeve's photograph also shows associates who need not have played on every track.

Rob Zacher: preliminary leads

Established from the photographed record and lyric sheets: Zacher is a named principal artist and producer, credited with cover design, and attributed with the lyrics and/or music of most songs. The lyric pages place this work in 1979–81.

A handwritten research note attached to the newspaper photocopy, in a hand and with a provenance not yet established, adds:

  • “Rob Zacher – Drama Teacher BCS 1979.” BCS plausibly means Burra Community School, but the expansion requires confirmation.
  • “[Won] Film-maker Award” — award title, date and film unspecified.
  • “Concert 4/12/1982” — venue, participants and documentary source unspecified.
  • “1982 – left to go to Reynella East” — this may refer to a school posting; independently unconfirmed.
  • “Copper Festival 1983” — relationship to Rob or the record unconfirmed.
  • “Married Rosie Allen 1984” — unverified personal note; do not publish as settled biography without a suitable corroborating source.

The note is useful as a research itinerary, not as a citation equivalent to the contemporary sleeve.

Don Griffen: the Mt Bryan family

A photocopied article headed “Burra Broadcaster Issue 465, 12 July 2000, page 8” begins by reporting that Don and Miriam Griffen, and their children Amelia and Edward, had moved to Blanchetown after a long association with Mt Bryan and the Burra district. It is not presented on the photocopy as an original signed interview; locate the newspaper issue to confirm its author, wording and any image/caption.

The family history in that clipping runs through three generations:

  • Thomas Patrick (“Paddy”) Griffen, described in the clipping's bracketed note as born about 1885 and dying 9 October 1969. The narrative says he moved to Mt Bryan after his mother married Billy Dare, married Hester Mildred Simmons, worked with a wharf-building firm in Port Adelaide and took over his brother Martin's Mt Bryan soldier's block after Martin died in 1930.
  • Charles Howard Patrick Griffen (“Howard”), Paddy's son, born at Birkenhead on 27 March 1917 and died in 1999 according to the printed bracketed genealogy. Howard worked on the family farm and served in the Second World War, rising to lieutenant. The article says he spent much of his service in the Middle East as a radio operator with the 9th Division Cavalry, then trained troops at Canungra, Queensland. He married Sylvie Irene Lloyd in 1944. (The article gives Sylvie's dates as 1923–1990.)
  • Don Griffen, Howard's son and one of the musicians on The Burra Boys. The article presents him as following his father's agricultural and community interests. It describes participation in Rural Youth, Apex, the Agricultural Bureau, Farmers' Federation, Burra Volleyball Club, Mt Bryan School Council, Hall Committee, “Jam with Ham”, Burra Tourism Association, Merino Capital Committee, the agriculture minister's advisory council, the Burra Show Society and the Goyder show/day committee. These are the newspaper's claims; do not infer the years or offices of every membership without additional records.

The article credits Howard with service to local organisations, the Burra Show and the RSL, and with innovations including an early fleece-weighing wool table. It describes Don as innovative in farming and sheep production: he designed and built a shearing shed with facilities for artificial breeding and, in the late 1980s, took part in research and development with the Department of Agriculture and the Collinsville Merino Stud. Distinguish these reported achievements from independently assessed inventions or firsts.

Don and Miriam married in 1982, according to the clipping. Miriam taught at Burra and Mt Bryan, and the article names her involvement in the Mt Bryan–Hallett Play Group, school council, Burra Guides, Crippled Children's Association, Callisthenics Club and Regional Art Gallery. Amelia and Edward's community involvement is also mentioned. Their individual personal histories are outside this album dossier.

The same yellow note used for the Zacher leads ends “Don pass[ed] 2016” (reading uncertain). This is not corroborated by the 2000 article, which could not report an event sixteen years in its future; seek an obituary or family confirmation before publishing a death date.

Notable gap: the 2000 newspaper article discusses Don's extensive community and agricultural work without mentioning this album or his music. It provides social context, not independent verification of a musical career.

The Burra depicted in the songs

The lyrics speak in constructed local voices. Their value extends beyond an inventory of places:

  • The mine and its machinery: Morphett Pumphouse personifies the engine building and names Morphett's shaft, a boiler hauled by bullock team, Schneider's stack, Peacock's enginehouse and Johnny Green. Compare the details with Johnny Green and the mine chronology; the singer's dates may be rounded or deliberately telescoped.
  • Workers, migration, and class: Copperhouse Blues, Jinker Song, Redruth Jail and Thomas Pickett imagine the experiences of the miner, drover or discoverer. Their first-person lyrics should not be mistaken for quotations from those historical people.
  • Places that changed use: Courthouse Hotel, Dugout Song and Tiddy Oggy mix local geography, remembered buildings and comic folklore. The narrated succession of uses of the Courthouse Hotel is a hypothesis for the building's history, requiring property and newspaper checks.
  • Country beyond the mining town: Hutt River Valley evokes itinerant labour, homelessness, grog and an enduring attachment to place.
  • The First World War and remembrance: The Burra Boys names the Burra Cheer-up Band, the Burra Brass Band, Beersheba, Gallipoli, a soldier memorial and “102 names”. Check the memorial's inscription and the identities of any named bandsmen against military and contemporary local records; avoid treating a lyric's exact number or named casualty as a primary historical fact.
  • Social history of memory-making: the lyric sheet's printed 1979–81 dates, the album's 1982 copyright and the possible school/copper-festival links together offer a promising history of how Burra residents made their own historical culture in sound and performance.

The Johnny Green connection

Morphett Pumphouse explicitly puts Johnny Green on the pumping complex. This is a valuable 1980-era instance of the mascot in local cultural memory and should be cross-linked to the Johnny Green article. It must not, on its own, arbitrate the sequence of wooden/iron effigies or the date of the original figure: the song was written more than a century after those events.

Lyrics are dramatic evidence, not literal witness statements

Songs such as Thomas Pickett and Stay Down Below compress mining history into speakers, rhyme, humour and moral argument. The Ballad of … is overt comedy and should not produce unverified person records for named characters. Descriptive terms like Copperhouse, jinker, sly grog, crib, poppet, launders and tiddy oggy are worth their own subject indexing and contextual glossary.

The transcription originated with reading photographs of hand-lettered pages, not with listening to the audio; it has not been independently proofread against a flat scan or recording.

Research agenda / verification work

  1. Preserve the primary objects. Scan sleeve front/back (including rear captions/credits), actual disc labels, and all lyric pages flat at archival resolution; record owner, physical format and rights/contact information. Keep the photographed 2000 clipping and sticky note as separate assets.
  2. Check titles, order and sound. Read the record labels for side/track order, run times and publishers; listen to the LP to settle names, dialect, repeated refrains, truncations and obscured handwriting. The current transcription should never be labelled definitive.
  3. Check the sleeve credits. Confirm exact spellings of Richard Lockhead and Gail Carragher and the publisher/label business history; look for catalogue or deposit records for Side Track TMS 008.
  4. Trace the makers' period in Burra. School records/staff magazines for Rob Zacher (BCS, 1979, alleged 1982 move); dated local gig programmes; the 4 December 1982 concert; the 1983 Copper Festival; award listings. Seek a direct oral-history interview where possible.
  5. Verify the Griffen genealogy and dates. Find the original Burra Broadcaster Issue 465, page 8, and an obituary or other independent source for the note that Don died in 2016; resolve what the newspaper's genealogical bracket was based on.
  6. Fact-check each historical song. Start with the mine and Johnny Green, the timing of the Victorian gold rush, Thomas Pickett, the Courthouse Hotel, Burra's dugouts, and the First World War memorial. Distinguish a factual correction to a song from a correction to a transcription: never silently rewrite the authors' lyrics.
  7. Rights and public display. Identify who can consent to digitised recordings, lyrics, photography and artwork. BHG/BDA may catalog and describe these items before permission for full-text/public audio republication is settled.

Further external research — 2 October 2026

Method and scope. This update uses (a) the original artefacts above, (b) independent catalogue/discographic and institutional sources, and (c) the Burra History Group's working research site and underlying public source repository. The working website is a source map and an interpretation, not contemporary proof for events in the 1840s or an independent eyewitness source for the 1982 recording. Its hyperlinks often lead to earlier newspapers and the Ian Auhl Collection. Source descriptions and original authorship must be retained when the material is reused.

Identification: independently corroborated record and spelling

  • Michael de Looper's Australian Indie Labels, 1955–1990, online edition, p. 186, explicitly lists TMS 008 — THE BURRA BOYS — ROB ZACHER & DON GRIFFEN — 1982 under SIDETRACK, described there as a folk label associated with Talunga Music. This corroborates the title, catalogue number, artists, spelling Griffen and 1982 date independently of the sleeve. As a discography it supports a release year, not a month or live launch date.
  • The National Film and Sound Archive's searchable Australian Vinyl Record Catalogue, Part 4, U–Z also indexes Rob Zacher, The Burra Boys, 1982, Sidetrack, TMS 008. Its index spells the collaborator's surname “Don Griffin”, unlike the LP sleeve and de Looper's Griffen. Preserve Griffen as the preferred artist form; log Griffin as a catalogue variant rather than silently treating it as a second person. The PDF's indexed text was located, but its full original was not consistently downloadable when checked; verify the original NFSA entry before treating any ancillary fields as authoritative.
  • A record-shop listing identifies a surviving second-hand LP copy under Sidetrack. This establishes that commercial copies have circulated, but stock and condition are volatile and a shop description is not session documentation.

Interpretation, not a production claim: the label's repertoire placed the Burra record within the South Australian and wider Australian traditional/folk market, alongside recordings by international folk acts. This does not prove that the label personally recorded all its overseas artists; some may have been licensed for local issue. Pressing company, manufacturing plant, distribution arrangement, accounting for royalties, first-sale date and label owner remain unverified.

Talunga Music. The LP sleeve names Chris O'Connor of Talunga Music in post-studio publishing. O'Connor and Suzette Watkins are independently associated with the folk tune collection Begged, Borrowed & Stolen, published by Talunga Music (first edition 1979; third edition 1981). See the Australian Folk Music tune-book index and a bookseller's third-edition listing. A period copy associates Talunga with Birdwood, South Australia, but address/precise corporate identity must be checked on a title page. The label catalogue plus sleeve and folk tune book make Talunga's involvement clear; they do not by themselves establish whether O'Connor owned the label, what the publishing contract said or who now controls master rights.

Studio 202 and Phil Hibberd — independent career cross-check

The Burra Boys sleeve credits Phil Hibberd, Studio 202, Adelaide. A separate account of Toyland's eponymous 1983 record on Austudy Records' annotated 2024 reissue says the Adelaide band worked for three days at Studio 202 with Phil Hibberd as session producer/mixer. This is independent confirmation that the named engineer and studio were active on other records in the early 1980s, including genres beyond folk. It is not evidence that the two bands shared sessions or that The Burra Boys was recorded in 1983; retain the sleeve as evidence of this album's credits.

A future oral-history lead is any surviving Studio 202/Hibberd archive, session diary, reel inventory, receipt or engineer interview. No public session log or exact recording date has been located.

Historical song cross-checks from BHG, museums and other external records

“Thomas Pickett.” The BHG mine chronology identifies shepherds William Streair and Thomas Pickett in the discovery of the copper outcrops in August 1845 and reports Pickett's poverty and death in November 1851; its history attributes two separate £10 payments to him. Compare these records with the song's simplified first-person account. The timeline is a BHG synthesis, so the next historical check should be original discovery reports and contemporary death notices, not retrospective lyrics.

“Stay Down Below.” The same mine chronology documents loss of Burra labour to Victorian gold in the early 1850s and the cessation of pump operations in October 1852. These independently support the broad historical setting of the song, without proving its exact figures or imagined speech. One should check the precise contemporary Victorian miners' licensing chronology before publishing factual commentary on the verse set in 1852–56.

“Morphett Pumphouse.” The BHG workshop's Johnny Green feature (underlying public source) sets out the distinction between the earlier wooden figure at Roach's and the iron figure on Schneider's and later Morphett's, moved to Morphett's in 1858. It dates the Morphett's fire to 5 December 1925, citing a 10 December juvenile-court hearing that corrects a 12 December date repeated in some later retellings. For the song, Johnny Green is a 1980s memorial tradition attached to real nineteenth-century machinery. Never identify Johnny Green with Thomas Pickett. Cross-link the main Johnny Green article and the BHG feature; use the underlying newspaper citations in eventual public-facing historical claims.

“Dugout Song.” The BHG Creek Street workshop feature (underlying source) quotes W. A. Cawthorne's description of a creek-bank “street” with above 1,500 residents in 1850; it identifies serious 1851 flooding, the South Australian Mining Association's subsequent prohibition on creek-bank occupation, later residents and another 1859 flood. These themes correspond closely to the song's imaginative domestic portrait and flood ending. Use Cawthorne, original press reports and the relevant conservation study for specific numbers and events; the site's secondary narrative cannot prove the lyrics' details.

“Courthouse Hotel.” BHG's historic places index identifies the Courthouse Hotel, Ludgvan Street, Redruth, operating 1858–1921 and with later non-hotel uses including a Methodist youth camp. Distinguish the hotel from the nearby historic courthouse. The verses' exact sequence of uses is not yet checked against land title and licensing records.

“The Burra Boys” — memorial and band. This has generated unusually good independently documented corroboration. The South Australian Governor's 2022 centenary address explicitly says 102 First World War dead are named on the Burra and District Fallen Soldiers Memorial. The BHG war-history page identifies the memorial's unveiling by Prime Minister W. M. Hughes in 1922, with those 102 names representing the wider district (Apoinga, Booborowie, Burra, Hallett, Hanson and Mt Bryan). The lyric's number 102 is therefore historically supported as the monument's original First World War roll, though its reported physical height is not verified by these sources. The DVA Anzac Portal documents the actual Burra Cheer-Up Ladies' Band, established to replace the depleted men's band and welcome serving men home. A surviving drum major's staff at Burra's Market Square Museum bears an August 1917 dedication. The SA History Hub history documents its appearances and 1920 winding-up. Chronology caveat: DVA describes the founding in November 1916, while the 2014 ABC report says first rehearsal in 1915; keep those differing claims separate until checked against contemporary newspapers. The broader ABC account of the Ockenden family independently reports 102 district men killed. A very strong publicly displayable object for the eventual article is the DVA-recorded 1917 presentation staff, subject to image reproduction rights.

The “Fred Wheatley” line is an identity problem, not yet a disproved lyric. The working transcription of the title song appears to refer to Fred Wheatley leaving the Burra Brass Band to fight overseas and dying there. A person indexed by the Virtual War Memorial as Frederick Wheatley, born 11 February 1879 in Burra, served in WWI (service no. 1056) but reportedly died in 1956, decades after the war. The BHG historical school register lists Frederic Wheatley with the same date of birth. That makes him a plausible search candidate but there is no evidence yet that this serviceman is the band's Fred, that the provisional lyric reading is accurate, or that the narrative was intended as literal death. Do not claim that the lyric is wrong or that the soldier and songwriter's character are the same person. Next compare LP audio, 1914–18 band rosters, casualty rolls, the memorial inscription and service dossiers.

A late-1970s songwriting context. The BHG town chronology notes copper-history commemorations and the revived Copper Festival of the early 1970s. Together with the photographed sheet's 1979–81 composition dates, these are evidence for the cultural setting in which local musical history-making was feasible, not proof that a festival commissioned the album or that the artists played any particular concert.

Cornish folklore and music existed in print before the LP. The BHG workshop's Elizabeth A. Ward article traces her 1936 Memories of Burra series, including workmen's songs, children's rhymes, mining machinery and dialect. Its “When I was a chicken” research tracks a Burra-adapted version of a travelling comic song. These establish a longer local habit of musical oral history, but no direct influence from Ward upon Zacher or Griffen is documented.

Album's comic winery ballad: guard against anachronism

The uncertain Ballad of [Harold Glee and Stanley Kanoon?] reading has phonetic similarities to Kilikanoon and Skillogalee in the Clare Valley. Do not identify the song's characters as actual wine-producers or assert the modern brands inspired it without hearing the recording or inspecting the physical title. Kilikanoon Wines' official chronology says the modern winery company was founded in 1997, while the documented property/name may be older: a 1981 song cannot be a straightforward reference to the 1997 brand. Verify the name and period before drawing conclusions.

  3. Seek histories or oral interviews for surviving Zacher, Griffen family, Chris O'Connor, Suzette Watkins, Phil Hibberd, Richard Lockhead and other sleeve-credited participants, carefully distinguishing role/career claims from shared-name coincidences.

Contemporary team and alternative spellings

A photograph accompanying a later group named “Jam with Ham” identifies Don Griffen on guitar, John Oates on drums, and Richard Lockheed on clarinet. All three names overlap the rear-sleeve's printed photograph caption, but appearing in both photographs does not prove those musicians played on every album track. BITN separately calls Richard Lockheed in an aerial mine-photography entry and “Coordinator Music Program” notice. Our initial sleeve transcription read “Lockhead”. Preserve the ambiguity until a clear physical sleeve scan; Lockheed now has multiple period witnesses. Period group roster.

A further 1980s Copper Festival programme extract bills the Burra Boys alongside performer Peter Coombe, soprano Lorna Orr and others. The generated date is unreliable; check actual festival year before scheduling a second post-LP joint concert. The local 1980 Courthouse Hotel tearoom enterprise is documented not only in BITN but also in a Greg Coote's original April 1982 photograph and first-person retrospective, “Tiddy ’oggey Tea Room, Burra 1982”. The Flickr photo page explicitly dates the image to April 1982 (uploaded December 2019). Its caption attributes the enterprise to friends, dates its opening to 1980, and describes the building's subsequent commercial changes; Flickr marks the image all rights reserved. Photographer recollection is evidence of memory, not an unambiguous contemporary title deed or the songwriter's own testimony; obtain permission for public reproduction.

Independent national artefact supporting the title track

The Australian War Memorial's P09291.245 “Burra Boys” photograph, circa May 1917, captures nine Burra district soldiers of the 32nd Battalion in France. The AWM itself uses the term “Burra Boys”, decades before the LP. It identifies individual soldiers and their fates and marks the image public domain. This makes a strong illustration of the period phrase, not evidence that Zacher saw that particular photograph. The 2022 SA Governor centenary address confirms 102 World War I names on the memorial, as the title song states.

Strongest sound-archive lead: the Radio 5UV Folk Co-operative tapes

State Library of South Australia describes its Radio 5UV Folk Co-operative Collection, OH 188, donated in 1994, including South Australian folk festival and Adelaide folk-club reel recordings from the 1970s and 1980s. Contemporary Burra publicity explicitly says Zacher and Griffen recorded some of their songs for 5UV. Ask the State Library whether OH 188's complete finding aid lists Rob/Robert Zacher, Don/Donald Griffen, The Dugout Song, the 1979 State Folk Festival, Traitors Gate and the Irish Club, and what access/copying rights are available. This is a plausible archive lead, not proof that the duo's own recordings survive in that accession. Other relevant holdings include Judith Crossley's PRG 1687/6 recordings of a 1979 5UV programme on Burra, No Songs for Burra—proximity in medium/date, not attributed Burra Boys material.

  3. Production archive: identify the 1980 Belle of Burra Burra programme/music score/cast list, original 1979 State Folk Festival award programme, and the 4 December concert's calendar year.

Further research, 2 October 2026 — Talunga's contemporary paper trail and additional Burra performances

The records below were located after the foregoing dossier was assembled. They materially strengthen our understanding of Talunga Music Pty Ltd as a commercial folk-music enterprise, and offer two new leads into the duo's theatrical presence. These are independent period ephemera or Eric Fuss's transcriptions of period local news, not evidence that anybody presently owns the LP's rights. Dates on the BITN BCS/CN import should be independently checked against the original newspaper, because its generated metadata has known contamination.

Talunga was promoting, publishing and presenting folk music beyond South Australia

June 1982: company identity and historical contact address. The June 1982 newsletter of the Folk Song and Dance Society of Victoria includes a whole-page advertisement for the Australian Folk Directory 1982/83, offered through Talunga Music Pty. Ltd. at P.O. Box 151, Birdwood, SA 5234; the period advertisement gives telephone (085) 68 5362. The directory advertisement specifically describes national listings of folk clubs, performers, organisations and festival dates. This is unusually strong contemporaneous evidence for the full Pty Ltd name, a Birdwood business correspondence address, and national folk-network involvement. The address and telephone are historic identifiers, not current contact details. Primary artefact: Folk Song and Dance Society of Victoria, June 1982 newsletter, p. 6 of PDF.

20 February 1982: touring/presentation link. The State Library of Western Australia, Festival of Perth Programmes, accession PR10960/1980–1989, records the Boys of the Lough at Perth Concert Hall on 20 February 1982, with Festival of Perth 1982 and Talunga Music Pty Ltd in the event's director/presenter field. The row is split across the library inventory's printed pp. 9–10; read both pages, not the standalone Talunga text on page 10, whose row continues from page 9. This corroborates Talunga's involvement with an international folk group listed on the Sidetrack label as TMS 003 in 1982 and TMS 009 in 1983. It does not prove the terms of label licensing, manufacture or distribution. Finding aid: SLWA PR10960 1980s listing, PDF pp. 9–10 (PDF page indices 9 and 10, printed pages 9–10); de Looper's updated Australian independent-label catalogue.

28 February 1986: a second institutional concert witness. Libraries ACT's Canberra Theatre Centre ephemera inventory identifies “Boys of the Lough in Concert”, 28 February 1986, with Talunga Music Pty Ltd and Canberra Theatre Trust as presenters; a newsletter is held in the collection. That confirms the company continued to present the group at least four years after the Perth engagement. It is not direct evidence concerning a later performance of the Burra Boys. Libraries ACT, Canberra Theatre Centre Ephemera, 1986 entries.

1985: overseas publishing credit. The original printed booklet of the **US Folk-Legacy album Fashioned in the Clay, by Gordon Bok, Ann Mayo Muir and Ed Trickett (FSL-104, 1985), credits the composition “Johnny Stewart, Drover” to songwriter Chris Buch and Talunga Music Pty Ltd. Its accompanying note identifies a connection with the Australian group Bloodwood**, whose Man Made Music is listed as Sidetrack TMS 002 (1981), and says material had reached the US performer via a musical contact. This confirms that the publishing name Talunga was printed on an overseas release in 1985. It does not mean Talunga published all songs on TMS 008 or that it owns its sound recordings. Primary source: Smithsonian Folkways-hosted original Folk-Legacy record/booklet, PDF p. 7, headed “Johnny Stewart, Drover”; de Looper catalogue.

Working interpretation: The LP's Sidetrack/Talunga connection was to a folk-music publishing, record-label and concert-presenting ecosystem with demonstrable interstate and overseas links, rather than necessarily to a small Burra-only record pressing. The business relationships between Chris O'Connor (named on the Burra sleeve), Sidetrack, Talunga Music Pty Ltd and successive rights holders still require company/contract research. Do not identify unrelated modern musicians called Chris O'Connor with the 1980s folk-publishing contact merely from a name match.

Further contemporary traces of Rob Zacher and Don Griffen

The 4 December Burra Town Hall show was probably in 1982. The previously identified undated advance notice advertises Rob Zacher, Don Griffen and friends for 4 December. A separately imported Burra Boys concert/photo item carries the explicit generated date 9 December 1982 and mentions a photograph of Shirley Jenner, Rev. Thiem and Sue Goodrich. Taken together these make Saturday 4 December 1982 highly plausible; unlike many neighbouring BCS records, the later item has a plausible, non-default 1982 date. However, the second item does not expressly give the show date or identify the photo subjects as performers, so check the original 9 December 1982 paper and its photo before treating the linkage as conclusive. This is stronger evidence than the anonymous yellow sticky note on its own.

A winery-ballad character appeared in a real procession. Eric Fuss's separate local-news transcript “Some Procession Highlights” mentions “Harold Glee's Limousine (Messrs Zacher & Griffen)” among vehicles/floats in a procession. In context, this appears to transform the comic figure Harold Glee from the duo's song The Ballad of Harold Glee and Stanley Kanoon into public pageantry. This is good evidence of a deliberate cultural crossover if the transcription is accurate; it does not identify the exact physical automobile, driver or character actor. The generated 1987 issue date is demonstrably unreliable for this corpus, so do not assign a year or Copper Festival number until the original procession report is dated. The song's title itself was already supported by the period concert publicity.

Further investigation prompted by these sources

  • Label and rights: research historic company registrations and archive records for Talunga Music Pty Ltd (Birdwood, P.O. Box 151 in 1982); look for a business successor, label contracts, publishing assignments, pressing invoices and surviving Sidetrack masters. The historical telephone number is a dating clue only. The 1982 Perth and 1986 Canberra programme collections might contain programme-book credits with names of organisers and owners.
  • Local performance history: inspect original Burra newspaper/BCS issues for the 4 and 9 December 1982 performance and the Harold Glee limousine procession; look for photographs, costumes, programme, and ensemble members rather than extrapolating them from a line of text.
  • Audio survival: retain the State Library SA OH 188 (Radio 5UV Folk Co-operative Collection) as the strongest plausible lead, because the duo explicitly told period press they had recorded for 5UV; an item-level finding aid or archivist's answer is needed to show whether those particular tapes survive.
  • Editorial scope: these discoveries improve the history of the recording and its makers, not the literal historical accuracy of every event narrated by the lyrics.
