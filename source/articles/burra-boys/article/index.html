Singing Burra Back to Itself

On Friday 8 April 1983, more than one hundred people gathered for the opening of the restored miners’ cottages at Paxton Square. Premier John Bannon was there, an official luncheon was held at the Civic Centre—and a local record was finally launched.

The Burra Boys, by Rob Zacher and Don Griffen, had already missed one advertised release. A week earlier, the local paper had announced that the album would appear at Adelaide’s National Folk Festival. It did not: on 7 April readers were told that copies were not yet available. The Paxton Square ceremony became the real launch. Bannon took a copy back to Adelaide, local station 5PI played the title track, and the first supply of autographed records quickly sold out.launch

The record that emerged that day was neither a conventional town history nor merely a souvenir. Its twelve songs turned Burra’s mine, machinery, dugouts, hotels, food, humour, pastoral country and wartime memory into performances. Some were comic, some mournful, some written in imagined nineteenth-century voices. Together they show a town singing its history back to itself during a period of renewed interest in Burra’s heritage.

A drama teacher and a farmer

The partnership began in the late 1970s. Robert Zacher arrived from Sturt College to take up a drama-teaching position at Burra Community School at the beginning of 1979. Contemporary school and community news identifies him first as a new staff member, then as one half of a songwriting partnership with Don Griffen.zacher

Griffen belonged to a long-established Mt Bryan farming family. A Burra Broadcaster profile published in 2000 remembered him as a farmer, community organiser and agricultural innovator involved in groups ranging from Rural Youth and Apex to the Burra Show Society and Tourism Association. That later article says much about his district life but nothing about the album. For his music, the record itself and the reporting around its creation are the stronger evidence.griffen

Zacher and Griffen wrote about the historical landscape around them. In 1979 they won first prize in the State Folk Festival songwriting competition with “The Dugout Song” and subsequently recorded songs for Adelaide radio station 5UV. The following year “Tiddy ’Oggey” placed third in the humorous-song section at the Mannum Folk Festival. In 1981 a dramatised performance of “The Ballad of Harold Glee and Stanley Kanoon” reportedly came close to winning at the Clare Folk Festival.competitions

Their repertoire extended beyond the eventual LP. At the 1982 Morgan Folk Festival they placed with “6 O’Clock in the Morning” and “The Nor-West Bend,” neither of which appears among the record’s twelve songs. They also performed at Adelaide’s Irish Club and Traitors Gate Folk Club. The Burra Boys was therefore the surviving selection from a broader period of songwriting and performance, not the whole of their work.repertoire

Songs in the town before songs on the record

The partnership was woven into Burra’s community life before the album appeared. In October 1980 the Burra Community Entertainment Group staged Belle of Burra Burra, a comic historical musical, to full houses at the Town Hall during the Copper Festival. Two Zacher–Griffen songs, “The Dugout” and “Copperhouse Blues,” were part of the production.belle

That same festival briefly joined a song, a building and a joke. Friends had opened the Tiddy ’Oggey Tearooms in the old Courthouse Hotel. Zacher and Griffen sang at the opening with the Roaring Jelly Band; the LP would later contain both “Tiddy Oggey” and “Courthouse Hotel.” The tearoom was not invented for the record, and the song should not be treated as a literal business history. What the overlap does show is how easily their material moved among stage, street, heritage building and local enterprise.tearoom

The pair also turned their comic characters into public performance. A later procession report lists “Harold Glee’s Limousine (Messrs Zacher & Griffen),” apparently carrying a figure from their winery ballad into town pageantry. Without the original dated issue we cannot assign the procession to a particular festival, but the reference reveals that the songs could escape the stage and become shared local jokes.procession

By late 1982 the duo had completed a recording at Studio 202 in Adelaide. A concert notice advertised Rob Zacher, Don Griffen and friends at Burra Town Hall on 4 December, and a separately dated report makes Saturday 4 December 1982 the probable date. The record sleeve names Phil Hibberd as engineer and Zacher and Griffen as producers. Hibberd and Studio 202 are independently documented on other Adelaide recordings of the period.studio

Twelve ways of remembering Burra

The hand-lettered lyric sheets date the album’s songs between 1979 and 1981. The sleeve carries a 1982 copyright notice, while the contemporary launch report fixes the public release in April 1983. Those are different dates for different events: composition, copyright and launch should not be collapsed into one.dating

The songs form a loose journey through Burra and its district:

  • “Stay Down Below” imagines miners leaving for the Victorian goldfields and the pressures of the licence system.
  • “Thomas Pickett” gives voice to one of the shepherds associated with the 1845 copper discovery.
  • “Copperhouse Blues,” “Jinker Song” and “Redruth Jail – Aberdeen Wail” inhabit a rough world of mining, heavy transport, drink, loneliness and punishment.
  • “Morphett Pumphouse” gathers together the mine’s engines, shafts, chimney, closure and the iron miner remembered as Johnny Green.
  • “Tiddy Oggey,” “Courthouse Hotel” and “Dugout Song” turn food, buildings and creek-bank life into comic or domestic local scenes.
  • “Hutt River Valley” looks beyond the mining town to itinerant rural labour and attachment to country.
  • “The Ballad of Harold Glee and Stanley Kanoon” is an extended comic encounter in the wine country.
  • “The Burra Boys” remembers district men who served in the First World War.

This is history in the form of character, rhyme and scene. It is not a set of archival depositions. “Thomas Pickett” can simplify a complicated discovery story; “Stay Down Below” can compress the gold-rush chronology; the Courthouse Hotel’s successive uses require licensing and property records, not merely a lyric. The songs matter precisely because they show what late-1970s and early-1980s Burra chose to remember—and how it chose to perform that memory.

That distinction does not make the historical details unimportant. Some prove remarkably exact. The title song refers to 102 names on Burra’s First World War memorial. The Governor of South Australia’s centenary address confirms 102 First World War dead on the Burra and District Fallen Soldiers Memorial, representing the wider district rather than the town alone.memorial

The song’s Burra Cheer-Up Ladies’ Band also belongs to documented history. The band was formed when wartime service depleted the men’s band and played for returning soldiers. A drum major’s staff presented in August 1917 survives in the Market Square Museum. Sources differ over whether the women first rehearsed in 1915 or organised formally in November 1916, but the band itself is not a songwriter’s invention.band

Other apparent details remain open. The working transcription of the title song seems to name a bandsman called Fred Wheatley who went overseas and died. A Frederick Wheatley born in Burra served in the war but lived until 1956. We do not yet know whether he is the man in the song, whether the name has been heard correctly, or whether the verse is being read too literally. Listening to the actual record and checking band rosters, service dossiers and the memorial are better next steps than declaring either the lyric or the archive wrong.

The mine as memory

“Morphett Pumphouse” is especially revealing because it places Johnny Green on the pumping complex alongside Morphett’s shaft, Schneider’s stack and the mine’s machinery. Written around 1980, it is evidence of the mascot’s place in local memory more than a century after the nineteenth-century events.

It cannot settle the object’s complicated history. Burra remembered an earlier wooden figure at Roach’s enginehouse and an iron figure first placed at Schneider’s, later moved to Morphett’s in 1858. The song gathers machinery, mascot and closure into one dramatic voice. Its historical value lies in that act of gathering.johnny

“Dugout Song” works similarly. W. A. Cawthorne described a crowded creek-bank settlement in 1850; floods and official attempts to prohibit occupation followed. Zacher and Griffen transform that larger record into domestic life beside the creek and an ending shaped by flood. The archival sources establish the settlement and danger. The song asks listeners to imagine a household inside them.dugouts

The LP belongs to a longer Burra habit of carrying history in speech and song. In 1936 Elizabeth A. Ward recalled Cornish recitations, mining vocabulary, workmen’s songs, children mocking mine captains and the sounds of industrial machinery. There is no evidence that Ward directly influenced Zacher and Griffen. The comparison matters for a different reason: both preserved a Burra that official chronologies alone cannot hear.ward

From Burra to an Australian folk catalogue

The rear sleeve identifies the record as Side Track TMS 008. Independent discographies confirm Rob Zacher and Don Griffen, the catalogue number and a 1982 date. One National Film and Sound Archive index spells the second artist “Don Griffin,” but the sleeve and another discography support Griffen.discography

Side Track was not simply a one-off Burra imprint. Its catalogue included Australian folk performers and locally issued recordings by international groups. TMS 008 sat among releases by Bloodwood, the Champion String Band, Denis Tracy and the Boys of the Lough. The sleeve also names Chris O’Connor of Talunga Music in connection with publishing after the studio sessions.sidetrack

Contemporary evidence shows Talunga Music Pty Ltd operating from Birdwood in 1982, advertising a national folk directory and helping present the Boys of the Lough in Perth. Later records connect it with concert presentation in Canberra and an overseas publishing credit. That places the Burra album within a wider Australian folk network. It does not tell us who pressed the vinyl, what any contract said, or who now controls the songs and master recording.talunga

The strongest lead to sound beyond surviving private copies is the State Library of South Australia’s Radio 5UV Folk Co-operative Collection. Contemporary Burra publicity says the duo recorded songs for 5UV, while the collection preserves South Australian folk-festival and folk-club reels from the 1970s and 1980s. No item-level match has yet been established. The archive is a promising question, not proof that a Burra Boys tape survives there.5uv

The record as an object

The sleeve presents Zacher and Griffen beneath the Market Square memorial and rotunda. On the reverse, a caption names John Oates, Phil Hibberd, Rob Zacher, Gail Carragher, Don Griffen and photographer Richard Lockhead. Other period references spell the last surname “Lockheed,” which has multiple contemporary witnesses. A sharp scan of the sleeve is still needed before its lettering becomes a definitive authority.

The production credits make the record a collective object even though two artists’ names dominate the front. Zacher and Griffen produced it; Hibberd engineered it; Zacher devised the cover concept and artwork; friends and associates appear in the photography and performances. The surviving lyric sheets allocate voices and instruments song by song, including guitars, violin, drums and pan pipes. A proper sessionography will require clearer scans and a careful listen.

That work also matters for preservation. The photographed lyric pages are not substitutes for flat archival scans, and a sleeve photograph is not a disc label. Both labels, runout markings, complete lyric sheets and the sleeve should be recorded at archival quality while the physical record can still answer questions that catalogues cannot.

Public access raises a different set of questions. A website may describe the album and quote modestly from it without assuming the right to publish a complete LP rip, full lyrics or high-resolution sleeve reproductions. Song copyright, master-recording rights, photography, artwork and ownership of the physical copy are related but separate matters. Finding a record is not the same as clearing it.

What survives

The Burra Boys survives as a record, a sheaf of lyric pages, local newspaper traces and the memories of people who knew its makers. Those pieces now reveal much more than a date and track list. They show Zacher and Griffen writing from within a period of festivals, school drama, folk clubs, historical restoration and renewed local confidence. Songs moved through competitions, radio, theatre, tearooms, concerts and processions before the LP fixed twelve of them in vinyl.

The launch at Paxton Square was fitting. Restored miners’ cottages stood behind one attempt to preserve Burra materially; a record launched at the same ceremony preserved it in voice, tune, humour and character. The two kinds of heritage were never identical. Buildings could establish where people lived. Songs could make listeners imagine what those lives felt like—and could reveal what another generation wanted Burra’s past to mean.

That is why the album deserves to be heard critically rather than treated as either quaint entertainment or literal testimony. Its facts can be checked; its inventions can be recognised; its makers and production can be researched. But its central achievement is already clear. Rob Zacher and Don Griffen made local history performable.
