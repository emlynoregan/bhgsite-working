That clip, from Arnold Laven’s 1965 film The Glory Guys, is the clearest sung version of the rhyme we can presently point a visitor at. James Caan’s Dugan starts at 1:11:11 on this upload: “When I was a chicken as big as a hen…” Another character asks whether it is an Irish tune; Dugan agrees. The question is useful. It is not a verdict.

The same skeleton is what Elizabeth A. Ward, writing in Burra in 1936, called the “sassy song of the mine boys.” This article is about that song: a playground and comic-rhyme family that reached Kooringa with local names swapped in. It is not a second Elizabeth Ward Feature, and it is not about Johnny Green. Ward quoted the song while looking at the miner figure on the engine house; the rhyme itself is about hitting captains back.ward-article

What the Burra boys sang

In “That there Man,” the first of her Memories of Burra, Ward has the metal miner tell of the engine, the whim, the dump, and the houses of mine officials. Then:

When I was a chicken no bigger ner a hen
Cappen Jinimy hit me
An’ I hit ’im agen.
Harry Cock came down to order me out
I up with me han’
An’ I give ’im a clout
Cappen Dick came down to give me a whack.
I had a stick
An’ I broke his back.ward-text

It is one continuous local performance. The chicken stanza is not a preface to an independent “control” rhyme. Each adult challenge is answered with greater comic violence, and the named men are Burra mine names, not generic parents.ward-article

Ward’s spelling is dialect on the page: “ner,” “agen,” “han’,” “Cappen.” We do not yet have a secure recording of her, or of anyone in Burra, singing it. The 1936 newspaper text is the Burra evidence. A diplomatic reading from the scan still belongs on the research dump.

A family, not a one-off

The rhyme is not a Burra invention. Folktrax catalogues “When I was a chicken as big as a hen” as a variant of the children’s two-ball rhyme “Over the Garden Wall” (Roud 19232). An Australian correspondent, Tricia, wrote in asking about her grandmother’s song. The grandmother was born in Australia in 1889; her grandfather was from Northern Ireland and her grandmother from England. Tricia’s text is the generic family, not the Burra captains:

When I was a chicken as big as a hen
Me mother hit me so I hit her again
Me father came out and gave me a clout
So I upped with the hammer and flattened him out
Me cruel mother killed me
Me poor father ate me
Me little sister sucked me bones and laid them on a log

She remembered it “sung to what sounds like a little Irish tune.” Folktrax printed the enquiry under “Over the Garden Wall” and pointed to field recordings.folktrax

Print is earlier than any of those tapes. In 1865 Alexander Smith put a drunk forgeman on the road in A Summer in Skye, singing:

When I was a chicken as big as a hen,
My mother ’ot me, an’ I ’ot her agen;
My father came in for to see the r-r-rrow,
So I lifted my fist, an’ I ’ot him a clow.smith

That is already an adult comic song in print, not only a children’s game. In 1892 G. F. Northall called the rhyme common in the Midlands and recorded a South Cheshire line — “My father came in, and he order’d me out” — that sits close to both Tricia’s father-clout and Burra’s “Harry Cock came down to order me out.”northall

The Burra boys appear to have kept the narrative skeleton and replaced generic parents with locally meaningful men. That is the local fact. The wider family explains why the skeleton was there to use.

What we can hear, and what we have not heard

Bill Smith of Bridgnorth, Shropshire, recorded in 1980, sings the chicken stanza inside “Over the Garden Wall.” The Musical Traditions notes call it probably a school-yard two-ball song, and (at the time of those notes) counted fifteen Roud instances, mostly from Scotland and Ireland.billsmith Geography in a catalogue is not the same as an Irish tune. It is another reason the Irish question is worth asking, not a reason to close it.

Folktrax’s One Two Three A-Loopa (FTX-201) includes playground recordings titled “Over the Garden Wall”: Peter Kennedy from children at Sidbury Church of England school, Devon, in 1951, and again at Kentish Town in January 1953. The same catalogue points to Damian Webb in Cumbria (1960–62), and to Croesgoch School near Fishguard, recorded in May 1982 and issued on Saydisc. We have the catalogue. We have not yet sat those tapes next to the film.ftx201

In Australia, Andy Rodoni sang “When I was a chicken as big as a hen” for John Meredith and Rob Willis on 10 December 1993 (NLA, John Meredith folklore collection). That recording is open for research. We have not heard it for this draft.rodoni

So the heard, citable performance for a first article is Dugan in The Glory Guys, from 1:11:11 on the YouTube copy above. Blu-ray subtitles place a substantial scene near 1:14:38; that is a different clock. Preserve 1:11:11 for this upload.glory

Two Irish memories, not an origin

Tricia’s grandmother (born 1889, Australia) remembered a little Irish tune. In 1965 an American Western independently has a character call essentially the same song an Irish tune, and the singer agree. Those are two memories that do not know each other. They make an Irish connection more interesting than a single family recollection. They do not establish that the melody is Irish, or that Burra sang the film’s tune, or that Ward’s mine boys sang what Dugan sings.

The next research step is listening. Compare the film melody at 1:11:11 with Sidbury 1951, Kentish Town 1953, and especially Croesgoch 1982; then with Rodoni 1993; then, if a Burra sung version ever surfaces, with that. Until those comparisons are done, this article should not be titled Over the Garden Wall on the hub. Ward’s name for it — the sassy song of the mine boys — and the first line, When I was a chicken, are the honest Burra titles.

The named men stay in Ward

Harry Cock can be placed at the mine with unusual precision (a 1869 Kooringa parody; a 1873 company-property house). “Cappen Jinimy” may be Captain James Roach; “Cappen Dick” may be Richard Goldsworthy. Those identifications are graded, not solved, and they belong with Ward’s biography and with the research dump. This article only needs the structural point: Burra localised a travelling rhyme onto real captains.names

Johnny Green does not gain a paragraph from any of this. The miner figure is the audience Ward imagined for the song. The song is not about the figure.

What this draft is not

It is not a claim that we have Ward on tape. It is not a claim that The Glory Guys preserves a Burra tune. It is not a claim that the rhyme is Irish. It is a first workshop Feature: Burra’s mine-boys’ song in a wider playground family, with a film clip a visitor can actually hear, and a dump for the catalogue, the timestamps, and the listening still to do.
