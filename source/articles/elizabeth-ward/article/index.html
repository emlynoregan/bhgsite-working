Prototypes: Burra in the News · Burra Digital Archive

In February 1885, a twenty-three-year-old Burra woman entered an Adelaide religious controversy. “There is one passage which I cannot let pass uncontradicted,” she wrote, signing herself “E. A. Ward” and “Salvation Soldier.” Fifty-seven years later, at about eighty, Elizabeth A. Ward sent “Australia Knows” from Hill Street to the Burra Record. Between those appearances she wrote songs, devotional poetry, memorial verse, family history, civic polemic and an extraordinary series of recollections about the Burra of her childhood.army1885

Ward’s enduring historical importance lies partly in the reminiscences published in October and November 1936. She remembered machinery, shops, schools, street cries, hymns, children’s games, comic performers, food, dialect and a night inside the smelting works. She preserved voices that formal mine histories rarely hear: mothers trying to put children to bed, girls reciting Cornish pieces, boys mocking mine captains, miners ordering provisions and a magpie imitating a shopkeeper’s call. These recollections belonged to a much longer public career in which Ward composed, argued, defended, exhorted and published.

Ian Auhl later reproduced some of her material but could not determine who she was. Eric Fuss, while compiling Burra In The News, proposed that the writer was Elizabeth Ann Ward, born at Clare in October 1861 and resident in Burra from 1870. The identification is not sealed by a single decisive document, but the accumulated evidence is strong. Ward’s dates, parents, school years, father’s occupation, Hill Street byline, religious service and obituary fit together closely enough to recover a life that had almost disappeared behind the initials “E. A.”1

This is therefore a biographical reconstruction, a provisional bibliography and a critical reading of a local author. Ward must be taken seriously without being treated as infallible. She wrote in her mid-seventies about a childhood six decades distant. Some passages are plainly eyewitness memory; some are inherited town tradition; some are shaped by moral nostalgia and familiar verse. Her unusual value comes partly from the fact that she often tells us which is which—and from the newly visible fact that she had been practising the uses of print for more than half a century.

The likely Elizabeth Ann Ward

Elizabeth Ann Ward was born at Clare in October 1861, a daughter of Richard Ward and Elizabeth White. Eric Fuss’s notes give two different days—10 and 19 October—and the original South Australian civil registration has not yet been inspected. The month and year are secure enough for the present reconstruction; the day is not.2

Her parents had migrated from Bristol aboard the Emigrant in 1854. Elizabeth later wrote to the Register about their voyage, naming Richard Ward and Elizabeth White and explaining that they had married at Trinity Church, St Philips. That 1919 letter is important beyond the family detail. It shows Ward acting as a custodian of inherited memory seventeen years before the Memories of Burra series.3

The family moved to Burra in 1870, when Elizabeth was about eight. Her mother’s obituary says Richard became a storekeeper and general dealer there. Ward’s own reminiscences independently place her in the same household. In “The Miners” she remembers children being sent to her father’s shop; in “Mr Coglin and the Billys” she identifies the call “Apple ho” as her father’s street cry, perfectly copied by Coglin’s magpie.4

A general dealer’s daughter occupied an unusually fruitful position. She did not grow up underground or in a mine captain’s residence. She encountered mining Burra through customers, errands, neighbours, schoolchildren and the landscape itself. Her writing crosses constantly between domestic and industrial life because her childhood did.

Ward dates her schooling precisely: “School Days 1870–1874.” She attended Mrs F. J. White’s school for girls and admits that the neighbouring boys often interested her more than the lesson. She remembered their calls and nicknames, the games played by girls, and recitations performed in Cornish dialect by Annie Ellery and Susan Jane Thomas. School was not merely where she learned to read. In her account it was one of the places where Burra’s oral culture was rehearsed and transmitted.5

Richard Ward died on 18 February 1886, aged 59. The contemporary notice places his death at Redruth and describes him as a resident businessman.richard Elizabeth’s mother remained at the Kooringa residence until her death in May 1911. The obituary names one daughter, Miss E. A. Ward, and a nephew, Salvation Army Major John Ward. No marriage has been found for Elizabeth, and “Miss” remains consistent in the surviving record.6

Her own 1952 obituary says she had been an active member of the Salvation Army in Burra for 61 years and had given long service as a Sunday-school teacher. Contemporary evidence now proves that the stated period is too short, or measures something narrower than first association. Ward signed herself “Salvation Soldier” in February 1885. In December 1886 she signed a local letter as secretary of the Burra Corps, and a report a week later said a Market Square meeting had been conducted by “Sister Ward, Hon. Secretary.” She was already an office-holder and public representative of the Army in her mid-twenties.army1885army1886

The Salvation Army context helps make sense of her public voice. Its culture valued testimony, recitation, hymnody, open-air speech and the moral use of print. Ward was not merely exposed to that culture. By 1894 the Burra Record was praising a song she had composed for the Christmas War Cry. In April 1895 it named two further poems, “He is Risen” and “Talitha Cumi,” and said the former was set to music. In October it praised “Australia’s Welcome to the General,” written for General Booth’s anticipated visit. The surviving notices place a young Burra woman inside a translocal religious print and performance network.warcry

Her work in the corps continued alongside her writing. At its annual social meeting in July 1898, “Sister Ward” sat on the platform with ministers and Army officers and opened the proceedings with prayer.army1898 In December 1902 the Burra Record reported that “Miss Ward, a local soldier,” had contributed a seasonal acrostic to the Christmas War Cry.warcry1902 Then, in June 1904, a local profile made the identification unusually explicit: the only daughter of Richard and Elizabeth Ward was “a member of the Salvation Army” and “a bright and earnest writer of poetry in the War Cry”; her contributions, it added, had also appeared in the Record.profile1904

The early records also strengthen the identification of “Sister Ward” at the funeral of John Gordon Fuller Elder in 1902. “Little Jack,” published in the same issue, commemorated the infant son of the officers commanding Burra’s corps. After an Army procession to the cemetery, Sister Ward spoke alongside Brother C. Morgan. The initials are omitted, but the documented secretary and War Cry contributor is now a much firmer identification than religious affiliation alone allowed. A week later the newspaper reported that the corps had commissioned photographic memorial cards. Ward’s verse belonged to a coordinated culture of communal mourning rather than an isolated literary submission.littlejack

Hill Street became her durable authorial address. An auction notice in 1913 listed Miss Ward as occupant of one of three four-room stone houses there, part of the estate of Jeffrey Pearce. In 1930 the town council gave permission for alterations to “Miss Ward’s house in Hill Street,” although the record does not yet show whether she had bought the same dwelling. Her bylines repeatedly sent Hill Street into print, making the address the base from which she intervened in the town’s arguments.hillhouse

In January 1945 the newspaper reported that Miss Ward, aged 84 and said to have lived in Burra for 74 years, had gone to live with friends in Peterborough. Her obituary says that for approximately her final six years she lived in a private nursing home in Adelaide, suggesting another move around 1946. She died at Melrose Park on 5 June 1952 and was buried at Burra the following day.departure The obituary printed her age as 92, although an October 1861 birth would make her ninety. This discrepancy is another reason to verify the civil entry rather than silently manufacture precision.8

A public writer for fifty-seven years

Ward was not a one-article informant discovered by chance. More than two dozen writings or publication events can now be identified, followed by the six titled sections of Memories of Burra. The list remains provisional. OCR often loses initials and verse; War Cry issues have not yet been inspected; the 1,000-copy edition of “Australia” has not been found; and a 1924 notice refers to an already published poem, “He Is Our Strength,” for which no original appearance is known. What follows is a map of a much larger career than the first search disclosed.

The Salvation Army print world

The four War Cry notices alter the starting point of Ward’s creative career. By her early thirties she was writing for a periodical that circulated far beyond Burra, and at least one text was a song while another was set to music. “He is Risen,” “Talitha Cumi,” “Australia’s Welcome to the General” and the 1902 Christmas acrostic place her work inside the Army’s characteristic mixture of scripture, testimony, popular melody, rally and print. The 1894 notice is grammatically ambiguous about whether the accompanying Christmas poem was also hers, but it unambiguously credits the song to Miss E. A. Ward. The 1904 profile supplies contemporary confirmation that these were not isolated appearances: Burra readers knew her as an earnest War Cry poet whose work also reached their local newspaper.warcrywarcry1902profile1904

This early experience matters when reading her later work. Ward understood verse as something used: sung, recited, sold, carried into mourning and attached to public occasions. Her poems were not chiefly private lyrics subsequently discovered. Even when conventional in diction, they performed social labour.

Her prose from the same period is just as revealing. In 1886, answering a complaint about the date of a Salvation Army tea, Ward explained that she had received Major Lindsay's orders and could not alter them. She then named her reason for breaking the Army's “usual custom of taking no notice of anything said against us”: if left unanswered, “such statements … would injure our work.” That sentence describes a habit visible throughout her career. Print was a place to correct the record.army1886

War poetry from the Boer War to 1942

“How the Highlanders Buried Their Dead,” dated February 1900, is now the earliest poem whose full text has been located. It imagines Highland soldiers burying comrades during the South African War, then turns to mothers, wives and children at home. The themes that dominate Ward’s later war writing—burial, bereavement, national remembrance and Christian reunion—were already in place.highlanders

Her First World War poems repeatedly approach combat through survivors. “One Fallen” and “Our Beloved Dead” are public elegies. “Mother’s Memories” follows a son from infancy and school to enlistment and death, insisting that death cannot undo maternal possession. “A Memory of Gallipoli” names its source as an incident recorded by “Trooper Bluegum” at the Dardanelles; its soldiers gather around “Padre Mac” and sing “Jesus, Lover of My Soul” and “Rock of Ages.” Ward was not claiming eyewitness authority. She transformed published testimony into devotional commemoration and told readers that she had done so.warpoems

The poems become most distinctive when public formula meets a remembered body or voice. “One Fallen,” written for J. Camp, narrows repeatedly to “One Burra boy.” In “Mother's Memories,” a mother sees first “a tiny form” in her arms, then a schoolboy, then the man who answers Britain's call; the final consolation is possessive and intimate: “Her boy is still her own, he's but ‘At Rest.’” “Our Beloved Dead” speaks in the voices of the soldiers themselves: “We are still ‘On Active Service’ … We have passed to higher duty, / And we serve a Greater King.”warpoems

The long “Gallipoli” of 1922 makes the memory project explicit. Ward addresses mothers as transmitters of the story, celebrates endurance by soldiers and animals, and treats Gallipoli as the scene where a nation was born. The politics are imperial and devotional: Australia answers the Motherland, sacrifice becomes national title, and the dead are secured within Christian eternity. Modern readers need not share that framework to recognise how consistently Ward helped construct local Anzac memory.gallipoli

She also experimented with form. The 1930 “Anzac Day” is an eight-line acrostic whose initial letters spell its title. Her 1940 poem places 1915 beside the new war: the sons of Anzacs face Hitler, the geography moves through Egypt, Palestine and Jerusalem, and the poem ends by opposing God’s eternal reign to dictatorship. “Australia Knows” in 1942 completes a remarkable arc from the Boer War to the darkest months of another world war.anzac3040

These poems are historically rich but not aesthetically uniform. They rely on familiar rhyme, elevated abstractions, Christian consolation and imperial language. Their very conventionality made them usable at public anniversaries. Their strongest passages usually become concrete: a mother’s remembered child, a burial party, soldiers singing at night, a horse or mule sharing human danger. Ward’s gift was less radical verbal invention than the conversion of public narrative into an intimate moral scene.

Print as service

The most striking evidence of material circulation comes from the Burra Cheer-Up Society. In June 1915 its executive voted to have 1,000 copies of Ward’s verses “Australia” printed and sold for the soldiers’ fund, and thanked her “very heartily for her contribution.” The poem itself has not yet been located. The decision nevertheless proves authorship, a substantial intended print run and a direct link between verse and fundraising. Somewhere behind the newspaper notice was a separate printed object—perhaps a broadside, leaflet or song sheet—that may still survive in a family album or institutional collection.australia1000

The 1911 elegy for “Viola” shows another kind of literary relationship. Printed in the Port Augusta Dispatch, it mourns a poet or contributor whose last poem was “Almond Blossom.” Ward praises a “Singer of sad, sweet song,” lover of tree, bird and “every living thing.” The piece suggests that her literary community was not confined to the Burra Record, although “Viola” remains unidentified.viola

Civic controversy

Ward’s letters are as important as the poems because they disclose the habits behind her historical prose. In 1885 and 1886 she defended the Salvation Army against statements she believed would damage its work. In January 1915 “Elizabeth Ann Ward of Hill St” defended Lindsay Kemble against a disparaging account of the masquerade that had brought him into trouble. She supplied mitigating facts about his pneumonia and theatrical work and described the episode as a clever joke. Whatever the merits of the case, the letter shows her publicly repairing a neighbour’s reputation.kemble

In January 1924 she objected to making ratepayers clean adjoining footpaths. Did the Corporation expect the residents of Hill, Ware and part of Commercial Street, “who are mostly widows and single women,” to form “a pick and shovel brigade doing work for which we pay heavy rates?” she asked. If so, “to what rate of wages are we entitled?” The comedy carried a precise claim about gender, taxation and municipal labour. Her August letter supporting a prohibition on Sunday tennis drew a pseudonymous answer from “Abooringa”; on 27 August she replied from Hill Street, noted that the opponent had withdrawn, and called the retreat “ignominious.” The signed follow-up removes the earlier uncertainty over attribution and shows Ward enjoying, rather than merely enduring, newspaper combat.1924

In June 1926 she proposed a house-to-house canvass to decide the fate of the Market Square guns, insisting that the parents of fallen soldiers had a right to speak. She wanted plates recording each gun’s capture, battle, battalion and division so the trophies would not become “pieces of scrap iron.” The 1929 poem “Let Us Forget” returned to the dispute; a week later she explained that the guns represented AIF sacrifice. These were sustained interventions in the public interpretation of objects, not casual letters about street furniture.guns1926guns1929

Her sharpest civic writing defended Edith Ruth Bubb. After Councillor Woollacott attacked Bubb for expressing an opinion about the trophies, Ward asked whether a woman required the council’s permission to speak. She called his attack “unmanly, disgusting and entirely unjustifiable” and said he was not free to assail “a woman of more ability and usefulness than himself.” Ward was about 68, unmarried, religiously active and living in a small town where public relationships were durable. She nevertheless rejected a councillor’s attempt to discipline a woman’s speech by name.10

The devotional poetry and praise of respectable miners’ homes must therefore not flatten her into a conventionally pious old lady. Ward could be loyal, combative, funny and severe. Her public Christianity did not require civic quietness; it trained and authorised speech.

Family memory before local history

The 1919 migration letter is biographically indispensable and methodologically revealing. It reaches before Ward’s birth through her parents’ testimony: their voyage aboard the Emigrant, landing at Port Adelaide, travel by bullock dray, primitive housing at Bundaleer, encounters with Aboriginal people, fire and flood, and later moves to Clare and Burra. The Register printed it and the Observer reprinted it. Ward was already turning family narration into public history seventeen years before Memories of Burra.emigrant-reprints

The details are the point. Ward remembered her parents saying that ship's boxes became chairs and tables, that for three weeks they lived on salt mutton, dripping and flour, and that her father killed fourteen wild cats with a waddy in one night. The letter also transmits a colonial settler account of Aboriginal people—sometimes observant and sympathetic, sometimes couched in the vocabulary and assumptions of its period. Because Ward was not yet born, these passages document family narration and settler memory, not her eyewitness experience. Their value depends upon preserving that distinction.emigrant-reprints

By 1936, then, the woman writing from Hill Street had nearly half a century of published argument behind her and more than four decades of located creative work. The reminiscences were not the accidental testimony of an otherwise silent witness. They were the late historical project of an experienced local author.

The shape of Memories of Burra

The bibliography of the 1936 series is unexpectedly awkward. Six titled sections have been located across five issues of the Burra Record. The final item nevertheless calls itself the “Conclusion of the series of four articles by Elizabeth A. Ward.” No instalment has yet been found on 20 October or 17 November.

The newspaper may have counted four commissioned contributions while using multiple internal headings, or the conclusion note may simply be wrong. Until complete issues are inspected, the responsible description is “six titled sections in five known appearances,” accompanied by the newspaper’s conflicting claim.11

The order is artful even if it was not entirely planned. Ward begins by looking upward at the miner figure above the engine house, then moves among mining families, down to the jiggers in the creek, sideways into school and town character, and finally inside the heat and noise of the smelting works. The sequence enlarges and contracts its scale repeatedly. Industrial Burra is recovered not as a system diagram but as an inhabited soundscape.

“That there Man”

The opening section begins with the metal miner later known as Johnny Green. Ward remembered him standing “in all his glory” above the large engine house with pick and gad in hand, but said she had never heard him given a personal name. He was “that there Man up to mine.”

Morphett's pumphouse and the miner figure later known as “Johnny Green,” about 1910. Ian Auhl Collection; catalogue record. This photograph post-dates the childhood landscape Ward described.

Her handling of evidence is worth noticing. “It is said” introduces the claim that a mine blacksmith made and positioned the figure. “I have never heard him named” marks her own recollection. The distinction between received story and personal knowledge is explicit.

For children, the figure was both landmark and supernatural actor. Mothers warned reluctant sleepers that the man from the mine would peer in at the window. Boys debated whether he descended when the clock struck twelve and how he returned to his perch. Ward stages these exchanges in eye dialect—“you’m,” “childer,” “mawther”—making voice central to the scene.12

The section then turns from folklore to vanished industrial grandeur and the houses of mine officials. Into this setting Ward places what she calls the “sassy song of the mine boys”:

> When I was a chicken no bigger ner a hen
> Cappen Jinimy hit me
> An’ I hit ’im agen.

The continuation brings in Harry Cock and “Cappen Dick,” each adult challenge answered with greater comic violence. The complete song runs to the breaking of Cappen Dick’s back. It is one continuous local performance, not a chicken stanza followed by an independent “control” rhyme.13

The underlying rhyme circulated in Britain before Ward arrived in Burra. Alexander Smith printed it in 1865; G. F. Northall called it common in the Midlands in 1892 and recorded a South Cheshire line—“My father came in, and he order’d me out”—that closely matches Burra. The Burra boys appear to have replaced generic parents with locally meaningful men while retaining the narrative skeleton.rhyme

One name can now be placed at the mine with remarkable precision. A local parody performed at Kooringa in 1869 names Harry Cock and William Hunn shouting triumphantly to their mates as the workings deepen and ore comes to grass. A 1873 assessment places Henry Cock in a four-room house on company property at the Mine. These records make Ward’s Harry Cock highly likely to be a real mine worker, though his exact occupation and authority remain unknown.harrycock

“Cappen Jinimy” may be Captain James Roach. A birth notice in 1862 explicitly locates Captain James Roach at the Burra Mine, and his 1889 memorial says he served more than eight years as surface captain. “Jimmy” is a familiar form of James, but no source yet supplies that nickname or Ward’s printed “Jinimy.” “Cappen Dick” may be Richard Goldsworthy: an early settler’s diary remembers “Captain Dick” as the underground captain, and mining histories identify Goldsworthy as a long-serving Burra captain. Both remain graded identifications rather than solved clues.jinimycappendick

The rhyme now has its own companion article, When I was a chicken. Within Ward’s work, its first importance is simpler: she preserved children’s mockery as carefully as she preserved machinery and hymns. The mine boys’ culture was history to her.

“The Miners”

“The Miners” is the richest social document in the sequence. Ward praises miners as hardworking and their wives as clean, thrifty and industrious. Those terms belong to the moral vocabulary of respectable reminiscence, and they should not be mistaken for a complete account of mining-class life. Yet the density of observation goes well beyond platitude.

She lists pasties, saffron or “seedy” cake, currant buns, apple pies and “thunder and lightnen”; remembers religious offices, choirs and anthems; evokes shops, Saturday-night music and children carrying their fathers’ cribs; and reproduces occupational and domestic speech. The repeated movement between food, faith, labour and performance reveals how little separation there was between “mine history” and household history in her mind.14

Her father’s shop is one node in that world. Children arrive with instructions spoken in dialect, allowing Ward to recall not only what was bought but how an adult sounded when ordering it. This is one of her characteristic moves: an object or transaction becomes memorable because it carried a voice.

Ward also knew how to end a social portrait with affection rather than abstraction. After defining a “gass,” a “jaw,” a “pitch” and a “tell,” she returned to the people who used the words: “Such were the Burra Miners in the ‘Brave awld days’ and many happy hours I spent with them.”

The section also discusses Canvas Town and the recruitment of miners. Here Ward is less certainly an eyewitness. Her family reached Burra in 1870, after much of the foundational migration she describes. The passage is valuable evidence of the story Burra later told about itself, but it requires corroboration before being used as direct evidence for earlier decades.

“The ‘Jiggers’”

The jiggers stood in the creek and formed part of the ore-dressing process. Ward says she watched them repeatedly. She writes from a child’s height, attentive to movement, water and bodily danger rather than to production totals or company policy.15

This makes the section a rare piece of industrial phenomenology: what processing machinery looked and felt like from the edge of the working site. Her account should be checked against engineering plans and archaeology, but such verification would complement rather than replace it. Plans can show the machine; Ward helps recover the act of watching it.

The section also demonstrates her compositional discipline. She does not pretend to have worked the jiggers or mastered their engineering. She locates herself as an observer and explains what remained visually memorable.

“School Days 1870–1874”

The school section is biographically decisive because Ward supplies her own dates and names Mrs F. J. White’s girls’ school. These details align the author of the reminiscences with the Elizabeth Ann Ward who came to Burra in 1870.

It is also one of the strongest demonstrations of her interests. “To me it was often more fun to watch the boys than play with the girls,” Ward admits. She records the adjacent boys' calls and nicknames, catalogues girls’ games and remembers Cornish recitations such as “When the Minister Came to Tea” and “Grammer’s Cat and Ours.”16

Hetty Symonds, whom Ward describes as a gifted dialect reader who died at twelve, offers a valuable future test. If Symonds can be independently identified in school, family, cemetery or newspaper records, she may both confirm Ward’s circle and reveal more about children’s dialect performance.

School, in Ward’s account, was divided by gender but joined by sound. Boys could be overheard; dialect pieces crossed from mining households into girls’ recitation; games created another repertoire of remembered words and gestures. Her historical method was already taking shape: she noticed the lesson occurring beside the official lesson.

“Mr Coglin and the Billys”

The Coglin piece is a town character sketch built from comic abundance: flags, goats, goat races, a ventriloquist’s jokes, family members and a talkative magpie. Ward says Louise Coglin still lived in the old home in 1936 and that 500 children followed Mr Coglin’s funeral procession. Both statements are specific enough to test.17

The magpie episode has particular evidential value. Its imitation of Richard Ward’s “Apple ho” call anchors the narrator inside the storekeeping family identified by other records. A comic bird thus supplies one of the strongest internal links in the author’s biography.

The piece also shows Ward resisting a narrow definition of historical importance. Coglin matters not because he held high office but because children knew him, public spectacle gathered around him and his household generated stories. Ward’s Burra is populated by memorable performers.

“The Burra Smelting Works”

The final section is the most intensely sensory. Ward says that, at about six years old, she entered the works on a Saturday night with a group of women holding a permit. She recalls furnace glare, sulphur, clanking chains, wheels, hammers, bellows, heat and molten metal.18

Ruins of the English and Australian Copper Company's Burra smelting works, about 1899. Photograph credited in the Ian Auhl Collection to the Tilbrook family and R. J. Noye; catalogue record. The ruins are shown decades after Ward's reported childhood visit.

The memory is organised as a sequence of impressions, much as a frightening or astonishing childhood episode might remain after nearly seventy years. It is compelling, but its date presents a problem. If Ward was born in October 1861, “about six” places the visit in 1867–68, before the family’s documented move to Burra in 1870 and near the end of Burra smelting operations. Possibilities include an imprecise age, an earlier family visit, a later family arrival than the obituary implies, or a different chronology for the operating works. The passage must be investigated, not rounded into agreement.

Ward closes with lines from Elizabeth Akers Allen’s “Rock Me to Sleep”—“Turn back! Oh turn back / Old time in thy flight”—without presenting them as original verse. The borrowing is a reminder that her memory writing is literary construction as well as testimony. She combines sensation, local tradition and familiar poetry to create an ending.

Immediately before that borrowed close, Ward supplies her own best account of her historical imagination. The visiting ladies could not have known, she wrote, that “a little girl standing so quietly by was taking it all in” and would describe the visit nearly seventy years later. Quietness here was not passivity. It was storage: heat, sulphur, chains, wheels, hammers, bellows, molten metal and human shadows held until they could be made public in print.

Voice, sound and memory

Sound is the most persistent organising principle across the series. The engine-house figure becomes real through mothers’ warnings and boys’ questions. Miners’ homes are recalled through instructions, hymns and music. School survives in calls, games and recitations. Coglin’s world contains ventriloquism and a mimicking magpie. The smelting works announces itself through chains, wheels, hammers and bellows. But these sounds were selected by a writer with long experience of song, recitation and public address. They are evidence arranged into prose.

Ward’s eye dialect will make modern readers cautious. It is not a phonetic record, and printed spellings may owe something to newspaper compositors. It can exaggerate difference and participate in conventions of comic dialect writing. Yet Ward does more than use a generic Cornish voice. She places speech in social situations, assigns speakers and remembers exchanges. Used critically, the passages are evidence for what a Burra woman in 1936 believed the speech of her childhood had sounded like.

Her recurrent position is neither centre nor complete outsider. She was not a mine captain, engineer or underground miner. As a girl she could watch boys across a school boundary; as a shopkeeper’s daughter she heard mining families without belonging to every household; as a woman she entered the smelting works with other permitted women; as a Salvation Army secretary she addressed meetings and represented an organisation in print; as an elderly writer she could preserve performances whose original participants had vanished from the record.

This oblique position is not a defect in her testimony. It helped her notice things institutional historians did not. Yet the new record also puts her nearer the centre of Burra’s civic and religious life than “observer” alone suggests.

Her moral framework is equally visible. She admires work, cleanliness, thrift, religion, family order and public usefulness. Nostalgia smooths some conflict and hardship from “The Miners.” But her 1930 letter demonstrates that respectability did not mean submission. She could defend women’s speech in sharp terms and condemn a councillor by name.

It would be too easy to turn this into a single personality trait, or to call the mine boys’ rhyme feminist because Ward later defended Bubb. A more modest connection is defensible. Across several genres, Ward valued vigorous speech: the sassy child who hits back, the distinctive street caller, the dialect reciter, the preacher, the poet and the woman who refuses civic silencing.

Memory, evidence and error

Ward wrote the series roughly sixty to sixty-six years after the childhood it describes. That distance demands source criticism, but not reflexive distrust.

Several internal claims pass independent tests. Her 1870 arrival matches the dates in her school heading. Her father’s shopkeeping matches her mother’s obituary. The “Apple ho” anecdote fits the same household. Her use of “Cappen Harry Roach” for Henry Roach shows that familiar forenames operated naturally in her retelling. The mine boys’ rhyme belongs to a traditional family known before her childhood, supporting her claim to have preserved an oral form rather than invented it wholly in 1936.

Other material remains layered. Ward could not personally remember Burra before her family arrived, so passages about earlier recruitment or mine administration must include communal tradition. The smelting-works age conflicts with the present family chronology. The exact dialect spellings passed through memory, manuscript and typesetting. Her praise of miners and their wives is both observation and retrospective moral selection.

One of Ward’s strengths is her occasional signalling of these layers. “I saw,” “I often watched” and “I was taken” assert experience; “it is said” marks hearsay. A close edition of the series should preserve those formulas rather than paraphrase them away.

Lost, quoted and found again

Ward did not disappear completely after her death. Ian Auhl quoted “That there Man” in Burra-Burra Reminiscences, but by 1987 he was asking publicly for information about its author. The writing had survived while the writer’s identity had detached from it.19

The Burra History Group’s four-page Johnny Green booklet, compiled by Julian Ratcliffe in 2008, again names Elizabeth Ward and reproduces a substantial passage from her 1936 article. It provides clear evidence of later reception: one vivid anecdote continued into public heritage literature.20

That survival was selective. The BHG booklet The Burra School does not mention Ward. The surviving digital material for the 1995 School daze! consists only of a cover and two-page foreword, so whether its missing body used “School Days 1870–1874” remains unknown. A physical copy in the Ian Auhl History Room or Libraries SA system is still worth checking.21

The pattern is historically interesting in its own right. Ward was not simply forgotten and then rediscovered. She was remembered in fragments. Johnny Green’s bogeyman story travelled forward because it fitted a durable town emblem. The larger writer—the woman who described industrial processes, preserved girls’ recitations, wrote memorial verse and confronted a councillor—became difficult to see.

Eric Fuss’s identification work reverses that process. His Burra In The News compilation should not be confused with the newspapers it indexes and transcribes: the newspaper remains the primary source for each historical article. Fuss’s editorial notes are nevertheless a distinct scholarly contribution and should be credited directly. They supplied the biographical hypothesis that the other evidence now strongly supports.22

The Ian Auhl Collection has a different provenance. It preserves the books, indexes, photographs and research record of historian and educator Ian Auhl and is housed in the Ian Auhl Local History Room at Burra Community Library. Public descriptions establish the collection’s location and character, but not a claim that the State Library of South Australia legally owns it.23

Why Elizabeth Ward matters

Ward gives Burra history something that mine statistics, company minutes and architecture cannot: a woman’s long practice of turning communal experience into public language. She tells us what children feared, repeated and mocked; how domestic labour and food sat beside industrial work; what a girl watched when she was supposed to be looking elsewhere; how a machine occupied the senses; and how voices persisted after the people and structures were gone.

She also complicates the category of “local historian.” She did not publish a monograph or claim scholarly authority. She wrote for newspapers, the War Cry and at least one separate fundraising edition. Yet her six 1936 sections form a coherent historical project. They select evidence, distinguish seeing from hearsay, name witnesses, reproduce language and move deliberately through the town’s social and industrial worlds. They came from an author already practised in argument, commemoration and the moral organisation of narrative.

“A Voice from Hill Street” names both address and action. Ward conducted meetings, composed songs, disputed critics, defended neighbours and women, raised money, shaped Anzac memory and finally wrote Burra’s industrial childhood into the record. Her work is not a transparent window onto the 1870s, but the late-life composition of someone who had spent decades deciding what public words should do.

Research status and next archival tests

The identification of Elizabeth A. Ward as Elizabeth Ann Ward is strong, but the following work would materially improve a final article:

  1. inspect the South Australian birth registration and resolve 10 versus 19 October 1861;
  2. locate Richard Ward’s shop and identify Ward’s Hill Street house through directories, rate books, sale records and assessments;
  3. examine Burra corps records and surviving War Cry issues for Ward’s offices, teaching, testimony, photograph and the full 1894–95 works;
  4. search for a surviving copy of the 1,000-copy 1915 printing of “Australia,” establishing printer, format, price and distribution;
  5. identify “Viola” and locate “Almond Blossom”; locate the earlier publication of Ward’s “He Is Our Strength”;
  6. inspect complete Burra Record issues from 13 October to 24 November 1936 to settle the page architecture, missing weeks and “four articles” discrepancy;
  7. finish the diplomatic reading of the chicken rhyme (now in the chicken-song dump) and keep testing Harry Cock, Captain James Roach and Richard Goldsworthy against mine, rate and family records;
  8. test Ward’s named children and townspeople—especially Hetty Symonds and Louise Coglin—against school, cemetery and family records;
  9. establish the chronology and operating status of the smelting works around 1867–70;
  10. inspect a complete physical copy of School daze! for reuse of Ward’s school reminiscence.
  11. confirm reproduction permission and preferred credit lines for the four embedded archival images. Digital evidence makes Elizabeth the strongest candidate for the photograph's “Annie Ward,” but no source yet establishes “Annie” as her familiar name; the identification should remain qualified unless newly digitised evidence resolves it.

None of these gaps prevents Ward from taking her place in Burra’s written history. They define the difference between a strong reconstruction and a finished biography.
