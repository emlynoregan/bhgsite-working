The Trees of Burra: From Treeless Plains to Garden Town

When the copper rush brought thousands to Burra in 1845, they found a valley almost entirely without trees. Over the next eighty years, the town's people planted their own forest — along the creek, in the parks, in the cemetery, and along the roads. This is the story of how Burra went from a treeless mining camp to a garden town.

A treeless valley

When the first European visitors came to the Burra Burra copper mine in the mid-1840s, one of the things they noticed most was what was missing. The valley in which the mine sat — on Ngadjuri Country, in a fold of the Bald Hills range — was almost entirely devoid of trees. A correspondent for the South Australian Register, writing in 1846, described the settlement and its surrounds in bleak terms (SA History Articles, No. 34 Burra):

The country around is extremely dreary. There are no trees, and scarcely any gardens, and the grass has a woebegone aspect familiar in such localities.

Another visitor to "the Burra" in the 1840s wrote that "all the buildings of this straggling town are, with the exception of two or three churches, bald and ugly." The absence of trees was not merely an aesthetic complaint. It meant no shade from the fierce South Australian summer, no windbreak against the southerly gales that swept down the valley, no timber for building, and no fuel — except what could be hauled from far away.

The pre-European landscape of the Burra district had likely been a River Red Gum (Eucalyptus camaldulensis) open woodland with a grass and shrub understorey — a description drawn from the Burra State Heritage Area fact sheet and the Heritage Standard for the Burra State Heritage Area. But decades of pastoral expansion, beginning with the first sheep runs of the early 1840s (Wikipedia, Burra, South Australia), had already thinned this woodland. The mining rush of 1845–46, which brought over 2,000 people to the valley almost overnight, placed demands on the remaining vegetation that it could not meet. Within a few years, the hills around Burra were bare.

Timber for the mine: the hidden forest

While Burra's surface was treeless, underground the mine consumed timber at an extraordinary rate. The Burra Burra Mine worked soft, clayey ground that required extensive and costly timbering to secure the drives and stopes. A visitor who went underground in the mine's working years described walking "through miles of these galleries without seeing anything but timber, and in some places the passages had been so narrowed with these supports that a stout person would find some difficulty in squeezing himself through" (Forebears, Burra Genealogy Resources).

The mine's appetite for timber was enormous and unrelenting. It was needed for shoring the underground drives, for the poppet heads above the shafts, for the launder supports that carried water to the waterwheel and ore crusher, for the engine houses, for the ore dressing sheds, and for the stables and workshops. The 1869 photograph of the mine on the Mine Timeline page shows "the timber in the fenced yard adjacent the mill" — stacks of cut timber waiting for use. Every year of the mine's 32-year working life (1845–1877) required fresh supplies of timber from somewhere.

Thomas Pickett, the shepherd credited with discovering the Burra Burra Mine, ended his days not as a miner but as a timber carter for the smelter — a measure of how important fuel and timber transport had become to the mine's operations. He died in November 1851, still hauling wood.

The Baldina forest and the wood carters

The first attempt at on-site smelting, led by Georg Ludwig Dreyer in 1846–47, was designed to run on charcoal produced from "the colony's forests." Henry Ayers, SAMA's secretary, advertised for 25,000 bushels — over 600 tonnes — of charcoal. But Dreyer's experiment failed, and the ore continued to be shipped to Swansea in Wales for smelting.

In 1848, the Patent Copper Company (later the English and Australian Copper Company) established smelting works at Burra, importing the entire works — men, materials, tools, and families — from Swansea (Wikipedia, Burra, South Australia). These smelting furnaces used coal, but they also required firewood for brick kilns, for the boiler, and for the firing-up of the furnaces. The company acquired what became known as the Baldina forest — a stretch of woodland on the Baldina Run, about 16 km east of Burra — for this purpose (Burra Mine History & Timeline, burrasa.net).

The Baldina Run itself was a major pastoral station of 50 square miles (130 km²), established by Henry Ayers in 1851, later leased by J. and C.B. Fisher, and then taken over by Alfred Barker (Wikipedia, Burra, South Australia). The Patent Copper Company, which held land at various points between Port Wakefield and Burra, grew hay at Kadlunga to feed its bullock teams and held the Baldina lease to supply firewood to the smelts (Burra Notables, burrasa.info).

But the Baldina forest did not last. As the local supplies were cut out, wood carters had to travel further and further afield — east of Burra, ranging as far as the Murray River — along roads that were often little more than tracks, difficult in wet weather and punishingly hot in summer (Burra Mine History & Timeline, burrasa.net). The bullock drays that hauled the wood were the same teams that carried ore to Port Wakefield and supplies back to Burra. It was arduous, marginal work, and it left its mark on the landscape: the wood cutters stripped the hills east of Burra as thoroughly as the miners and pastoralists had stripped the valley.

The open-cut and the recovered timber

In 1868, the mining engineer John Darlington arrived to assess the Burra mine's prospects. He found a mine "in soft clayey ground that needed extensive and costly timbering" and recommended open-cut mining as an economic alternative. The directors accepted the recommendation, and in 1870 Australia's first open-cut mining operation began (Mine Timeline).

The open-cut had an unexpected benefit. As the enormous excavation proceeded, the old underground workings were exposed — and with them, the timber that had been used to shore them up decades earlier. This recovered timber, still sound after years underground, was repurposed: it was used to fuel the pump engine. In one six-month period, the open-cut yielded 1,156 tons of old timber alongside 22,790 tons of deads and 26,382 tons of ore-bearing material. The mine, in effect, recycled its own forest.

The open-cut operated from 1870 to 1877, when the mine closed. The great pit remained — a vast, raw wound in the valley floor — and the hills around it remained bare. The era of industrial timber consumption was over, but the era of planting had not yet begun.

The planting begins: 1880–1906

The first recorded tree-planting in Burra came in 1880, when trees were planted along Burra Creek. Two years later, in 1882, trees were planted around Victoria Park (Burra Town Timeline, burrasa.net). These were practical as well as ornamental plantings: shade, windbreak, and the stabilisation of creek banks that had been eroding since the dugout era. The town was still small — the 1873 assessment had counted about 365 houses in Kooringa — but it was beginning to think of itself as a place that might outlast the mine.

The planting of the 1880s and 1890s was part of a wider colonial movement. Across South Australia and the other Australian colonies, towns that had been built in denuded landscapes were beginning to green themselves. The colonial government, through the Woods and Forests Department, supplied trees at nominal cost, and local councils and progress associations organised working bees. In Burra, the Corporation that had been formed in 1876 from the remaining townships took the lead.

The plantings were not universally welcomed. To some residents, the expense of trees and the water they required was a frivolity in a town that had only had piped water since 1884 — and even then, only to 100 houses (Wikipedia, Burra, South Australia). But the trees took hold, and within a generation they had changed the look of the town. The visitor who in the 1840s had complained of "no trees, and scarcely any gardens" would have found a very different Burra by 1900.

Jubilee Avenue

In 1887, trees were planted along Jubilee Avenue, between Kooringa and Redruth (Burra Town Timeline, burrasa.net). The planting marked the Golden Jubilee of Queen Victoria, who had ascended to the throne in 1837. Jubilee Avenue became one of the most distinctive landscape features of Burra — a line of mature trees connecting the two oldest townships, softening what had been a bare, windswept stretch of road.

A fountain was also erected in Jubilee Walk, and the name "Jubilee" attached itself to several features of the town, including the Jubilee Show on the new showground (Burra Town Timeline, burrasa.net). The 1887 plantings were part of a burst of civic improvement in Burra in the late 1880s — the first bicycle club was formed the same year — that spoke to a town growing confident in its post-mining identity.

The avenue was not just decorative. It provided shade for travellers and livestock moving between Kooringa and Redruth, and it marked the road as a civic space rather than a mere track. In a town where the absence of trees had been a mark of the mining camp's rawness, the avenue was a declaration that Burra was now a settled place.

The avenue to the cemetery

In 1906, an avenue of trees was planted leading to the Burra Cemetery (Burra Town Timeline, burrasa.net). The cemetery, on the hill above the town, had been in use since the early mining days, and the bridge over Burra Creek on the road to it had been built by public subscription in 1868 — supervised by D. Wells, the cemetery's curator — to make funerals easier.

The planting of the cemetery avenue followed a tradition common across Australia and the British Empire: avenues of trees as living memorials, marking the path to the dead. In Burra's case, the avenue also served a practical purpose, providing shade for mourners who walked or rode to the cemetery for funerals. The trees were a gesture of respect — and of permanence, a signal that the town and its dead were there to stay.

"Locality of sheoak": what's in a name?

There is an irony in Burra's treeless origins, because the name of its principal township, Kooringa, may itself refer to trees. Rodney Cockburn, in his 1908 What's in a Name? Nomenclature of South Australia, gave the meaning of "Kooringa" as "locality of sheoak." The sheoak (Allocasuarina species) is a native tree common in the drier parts of South Australia, and its presence would have been one of the few woody plants in the pre-mining valley.

An alternative etymology, cited in the township history on this site, derives the name from the Aboriginal word kuri-ngga, meaning "in the circle" — referring to a dance among the northern tribes. Both meanings are plausible; neither can be proven definitively. But if Cockburn's etymology is correct, then the name Kooringa is a ghost of the valley's lost woodland: a reminder that before the copper rush, the sheoaks stood here, and that the town which replaced them carries their name.

The connection is not just etymological. The Burra Creek, which runs through the valley, is named on early maps alongside Baldina Creek and Gum Creek — each of them a watercourse that would once have been lined with River Red Gums and sheoaks (Wikipedia, Burra, South Australia). The near-total removal of these trees in the first decade of mining was one of the most dramatic ecological transformations in colonial South Australia.

The Burra State Heritage Area and the landscape today

In the 20th century, the trees planted in the 1880s and 1900s matured, and new plantings continued. The Burra State Heritage Area, gazetted to protect the town's heritage values, recognises the landscape character of Burra as "a key part of its heritage value, defined by its natural topography, water courses and vegetation as well as its remnant historic mining activity and settlement pattern."

The Heritage Standard for the Burra State Heritage Area notes that "prior to European settlement, the landscape was most likely a River Red Gum open woodland with a mix of grass and shrub understorey, little of which remains." It acknowledges that "Burra's introduced and regenerated vegetation plays an important role in defining the character of the town" and calls for the maintenance of "established trees and an absence of developed street amenities" to reinforce "the historic rural settlement character of Burra." The Burra Smelts heritage study (SA Department for Environment and Heritage) documents the smelting works that consumed the Baldina forest.

Today, the visitor to Burra sees a town of mature trees — pines along the creek, exotic shade trees in the parks, and the remnant avenues along the old roads. The contrast between the treeless mining camp of the 1840s and the green town of today is one of the most striking things about the place. It is a transformation that was not accidental: it was the work of generations of Burra residents who decided that a town without trees was not a town worth living in, and who set about planting one.
